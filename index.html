01 — The film

A quiet story about grief, instinct and survival.

Two Rats is a 2D hand-drawn animated short film set over a single foggy winter night on the streets of a city that is usually calm after dark.

Outside a housing society, a mother rat is crushed beneath the reversing wheels of a car. Her two sons are left behind on the cold concrete: an older rat who understands that they cannot stay, and a younger one who does not understand what has happened and clings to his dead mother.

The older brother tries to pull him away. He calls, pushes and pleads. The younger one resists, weeps and watches his brother do something he cannot fully understand — something that finally reaches him. He gets up. He follows.

They find shelter in a small hollow near a tree in a park. The younger rat closes his eyes. The older one stays awake beside him, watching the dark. Before dawn, he returns to the street where their mother lay. There is almost nothing left. A cat disappears around the corner. He runs back, presses close against his brother, and finally closes his eyes too.

“We wanted rats to behave like rats, not like characters performing emotion for an audience.”

The film has no dialogue. We wanted the animals to feel like animals going through something they have no words for. To understand their movement and behaviour, we reached out to people who keep pet rats. They shared videos and recordings and helped us understand how rats move, react and comfort each other. We read, watched and observed wherever we could.

02 — The world

A whole city, carrying on around them.

The world of Two Rats is built with the same attention as the animals. Fog, hard yellow streetlights, concrete, winter cold — and the small traces of people living through the night.

A tarpaulin shelter on a footpath. A bonfire near the society gate where a security guard keeps warm. A woman visible through a window, finishing her kitchen work before the light goes off. None of it is explained. It simply exists at the edges of the frame, the way it would on an ordinary night.

03 — Where it began

Where do they go after this?

The film began with something that actually happened.

Rats used to get into my home. I caught a few in a cage and released them in a nearby park — the same park that appears in the film. I watched them through the whole process. They were afraid and trembling. When I opened the cage, they simply ran.

I couldn't stop thinking about it. Where do they go after this? Did they have someone waiting for them? Could they have a family?

That thought kept growing until it became this story.

The budget was small, so I kept the film short — five minutes at most. But the length was never the point. The point was to make something where you don't need to be told what to feel.
